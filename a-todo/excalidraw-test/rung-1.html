Primer

The one mental model: your search command is the whole field in miniature

Run bm search "query" and this happens (src/search_cmd.ts:59-78):

Two independent retrieval engines — dense (meaning-based) and lexical (word-based) — each produce a ranked list; Reciprocal Rank Fusion merges them by position. Everything in this rung is understanding why each box exists, what it’s good and bad at, and what the two boxes you don’t have (ANN index, cross-encoder reranker) would add. Hybrid is your default mode; keyword and semantic run each engine alone (src/search_cmd.ts:64-68).

What an embedding actually is

An embedding model takes a piece of text and returns “an array of floating point numbers” of fixed length regardless of input size — Willison: the array will “always be the same length, no matter how long the content is” (Willison, Embeddings · raw dump). Think of those numbers as “co-ordinates in a very weird multi-dimensional space” where “location…represents the semantic meaning” of the text. One property drives every use: texts that are near each other in this space are semantically similar — even when they share no words.

That’s the entire trick behind what Willison calls “vibes-based search”: his example query “sqlite backup tools” surfaces relevant posts that never contain the word “backups” (same source). Contrast with keyword search, which can only match literal tokens — the complementary failure modes are the reason your lab runs both engines.

Your lab’s concrete instance: gemini-embedding-001, asked for 768 dimensions via outputDimensionality (src/embed.ts:15-20, src/config.ts — EMBED_DIMS = 768), stored per note in a F32_BLOB(768) column (src/db.ts:48). One vector per note — that granularity decision is the chunking section’s subject.

Cosine: how “near” is measured

Similarity between two vectors is almost always cosine similarity: the dot product of the two vectors divided by the product of their magnitudes — which measures the angle between them, not their lengths. Willison’s reference implementation is exactly that formula: dot_product / (magnitude_a * magnitude_b) (Willison · dump). Identical direction → 1.0; his worked example scores ~0.88 for a strongly related article. Because only direction carries meaning, cosine ignores vector magnitude entirely — hold that thought for the normalization subtlety below.

Your lab computes cosine distance, not similarity: libSQL’s vector_distance_cos is documented as “Cosine distance (1 - cosine similarity)” with range 0 → 2 — “close to 0 indicates vectors are nearly identical… close to 1… orthogonal… close to 2… opposite directions” (Turso/libSQL docs · dump). Lower = more similar, which is why vectorSearch orders ASC (src/db.ts:146-156) — the docs’ own example does the same. When you read a score=0.4123 line from bm search --mode semantic, you’re reading a distance: similarity ≈ 1 − 0.4123.

The normalization subtlety (a real almost-bug worth understanding)

Google trained gemini-embedding-001 with Matryoshka Representation Learning (MRL) — the model “learn[s] high-dimensional embeddings that have initial segments (or prefixes) which are also useful, simpler versions,” so you can keep just the first 768 of 3072 dimensions (Gemini embeddings doc · dump, near-verbatim excerpt). The documented quality cost at 768 is nearly nil: mean scores on MTEB (a standard benchmark suite that averages embedding quality across many tasks — higher is better) are 68.17 @ 1536 vs 67.99 @ 768, degrading for real only by 256 (66.19) and 128 (63.31) (same dump). That’s why your EMBED_DIMS = 768 with the comment “plenty at this scale” is a well-supported choice (src/config.ts).

But the same doc says: the default 3072-dim output is unit-normalized, while truncated dimensions are not — “you must manually normalize non-3072 dimensions” (same dump). Your lab stores the 768-dim vectors raw — there is no normalization anywhere in src/embed.ts or src/db.ts.

Is that a bug? No — and knowing why is the lesson (local inference, ours — premises above): cosine divides by both magnitudes, so scaling a vector changes nothing about its cosine distance to anything else. Google’s “must normalize” matters for dot-product or Euclidean comparisons, where magnitude leaks into the score. Your pipeline is magnitude-immune because every comparison goes through vector_distance_cos. If anyone ever swaps that function for a dot product (a common “optimization” — dot of unit vectors equals cosine, but only for unit vectors), the missing normalization becomes a live bug. You’ll verify this empirically in the build.

Task types: queries and documents are different kinds of text

Your embed() takes a TaskType — documents are embedded with RETRIEVAL_DOCUMENT at index time (src/index_cmd.ts:61), queries with RETRIEVAL_QUERY at search time (src/search_cmd.ts:67,72). Why: a query (“best sqlite backup approach?”) and a document (three paragraphs about a backup tool) are different shapes of text that should land near each other when they’re about the same thing. Gemini exposes this as task types and warns “it is important that the task is used consistently” (Gemini doc dump); the full table also pairs QUESTION_ANSWERING and FACT_VERIFICATION query-side types with RETRIEVAL_DOCUMENT corpus embeddings. This is the same idea as the query: / passage: prefixes Willison used with E5-large-v2 (Willison dump) — your embed.ts header comment even says so (“the Gemini equivalent of getting nomic’s prefixes right”, src/embed.ts:1-3). Asymmetric embedding is the norm in retrieval, and mixing the sides up silently degrades ranking.

Lexical scoring: TF-IDF → BM25, the 50-year-old engine that still matters

First, the name in the header: TF-IDF scores a document by term frequency (how often a query word appears in it) weighted by inverse document frequency (how rare that word is across the corpus); BM25 is its refined successor — the same two ideas plus term-frequency saturation and length normalization. Keyword search ranks documents by combining three signals (Elastic, Practical BM25 Part 2 · dump, excerpt+reconstruction — the formula on the page is an image, reconstructed to standard form in the dump):

  1. IDF — rare words carry the signal. IDF(qᵢ) = ln((docCount − f(qᵢ) + 0.5)/(f(qᵢ) + 0.5)). Elastic’s worked example: a term appearing in all 4 docs scores IDF ≈ 0.074 — nearly nothing — while a term in only 2 of 4 docs scores higher. A word every bookmark contains (“the”, or in your corpus probably “AI”) contributes almost zero ranking signal.
  2. Term-frequency saturation — k1. More occurrences help, with diminishing returns approaching an asymptote. Elastic’s increments with k1=10: 1st occurrence +0.0741, 2nd +0.0618, 3rd +0.0523 (slow saturation); with k1=0.01 the 2nd occurrence adds only +0.00037 (instant saturation). k1 sets how fast “mentions it a lot” stops mattering.
  3. Length normalization — b (0→1). b=1 fully penalizes documents longer than the average; b=0 ignores length. With b high, match density (matched terms ÷ length) drives ranking, so a short note that’s all about your query beats a long note that mentions it once.

Your lab gets all of this from SQLite FTS5’s built-in bm25() with k1 = 1.2 and b = 0.75 hard-coded (SQLite FTS5 docs · dump, high-fidelity excerpt). One quirk you must know to read your own output: FTS5 “multiplies the result by -1 before returning it, ensuring that better matches are assigned numerically lower scores” — so BM25 scores in your lab are negative, best-first means ORDER BY … ASC (src/db.ts:170-174 does exactly that), and a score=-8.1 beats a score=-2.3.

Two lab-specific details (src/db.ts): the FTS index covers title, author, summary, tags, body (path is unindexed), all columns at default weight 1.0; and toMatch() (lines 160-163) lowercases the query, strips punctuation, and joins tokens with OR inside quotes — because FTS5’s MATCH syntax treats bare punctuation as operators and would otherwise throw. Cost of that choice (app fact + local inference): an OR-of-tokens query matches documents containing any query word, leaning on BM25’s IDF to keep common-word matches from ranking high — a phrase like “context rot” matches every note containing just “context”.

Dense vs lexical: complementary failure modes, hence hybrid

Neither engine dominates. Dense retrieval finds vocabulary-mismatched content (the “backups” example) but is weak at exact identifiers, names, and rare tokens — and your corpus is full of those (tool names, personal names, bm, FTS5). Lexical BM25 nails exact tokens and fails at synonyms/paraphrase. Weaviate’s framing of why hybrid exists: combine “the accuracy of keyword-based search with the semantic understanding of vector search” (Weaviate, Hybrid Search Explained · dump, excerpt+reconstruction). This is capability Q4’s seed: when should you use which — and the honest foundations-level answer is “keyword for identifier-shaped queries, semantic for concept-shaped queries, hybrid when you don’t know” — which you will test on your own corpus in the build, and study properly in rung-3.

RRF: fusing two rankings without comparing their scores

The two engines’ scores are incomparable — BM25 emits negative unbounded numbers, cosine distance lives in [0,2]. RRF sidesteps normalization entirely by using only each document’s rank position in each list:

RRF score(d) = Σ over lists 1/(k + rank(d))

(Weaviate dump, formula attributed to Benham & Culpepper 2018.) Weaviate’s worked example, with k=0: doc A = BM25 rank 1 + dense rank 3 → 1/1 + 1/3 = 1.33; doc B = rank 2 + rank 1 → 1.5; doc C = rank 3 + rank 2 → 0.83; final order B > A > C. The lesson in those three numbers: RRF rewards documents that rank decently in both lists over documents that top one list and miss the other.

Your rrf() is thirteen lines implementing exactly that with k = 60 — the conventional smoothing constant (the article’s k=0 is pedagogical; that k=60 is the default in common implementations such as Elasticsearch is general-practice knowledge, ours to flag — not from the cited page) — as 1/(k + i + 1) with zero-indexed i, i.e. rank 1 contributes 1/61 (src/rrf.ts:7-19). Its file comment states the why precisely: “merge ranked lists by position, not by raw score (so BM25’s negative scores and cosine distance never need to be normalized together).” What k does (local inference from the formula): with k=60, ranks 1 and 5 contribute 1/61 vs 1/65 — a small gap — so k dampens how much winning the top slot matters; with k=0 rank 1 is worth 3× rank 3. Note also search_cmd.ts widens the candidate pool before fusing — pool = max(limit*3, 20) per engine (src/search_cmd.ts:70-77) — because a doc at rank 15 in both lists may still deserve a top-10 hybrid slot.

One fidelity flag: Weaviate’s page also offers relativeScoreFusion (score-normalizing fusion, and an alpha weight: 0 = pure keyword, 1 = pure vector, default 0.75); the dump could not capture its internals verbatim and marks them as to-verify — treat the existence of score-based fusion as sourced, its mechanics as unverified (dump).

ANN: the index you deliberately don’t have

Your vectorSearch is a brute-force scan: cosine distance against every row, ORDER BY — O(N) per query, with the in-code comment “trivially fast at this scale, no ANN index needed” (src/db.ts:145-156). At ~450 notes that’s the right call, and Willison agrees at much larger scale: brute-force in SQLite is fine to ~20k items, “I’m not convinced you need an entirely new database” (Willison dump).

What an Approximate Nearest Neighbor index buys when N grows: sublinear query time, paid for with recall — it may miss true neighbors. Pinecone’s HNSW numbers make the trade concrete: recall ranging from ~80% at 1ms up to 100% at 50ms per 1,000 queries (Sift1M; the dump’s phrasing leaves ambiguous whether those times are per query or per 1,000-query batch), with memory scaling in the graph’s connectivity parameter M (Pinecone, HNSW · dump, excerpt+reconstruction). HNSW’s intuition: a layered graph with “longer edges in the highest layers (for fast search) and shorter edges in the lower layers (for accurate search)” — enter at the sparse top, greedily hop toward the query, drop a layer, repeat to the dense bottom. Global-first, then local-refine; built from skip-list + navigable-small-world ideas with (poly)logarithmic search complexity.

When your corpus outgrows brute force, the upgrade is one function away in the same database: libSQL ships libsql_vector_idx + vector_top_k (Turso docs dump) — the day you reach for it, you accept the recall/speed trade above and it becomes a versioned component of your pipeline (rung-6’s territory).

Cross-encoders: the judge your pipeline doesn’t have yet

Everything above is bi-encoder retrieval: query and document are embedded independently, and similarity is computed between the two frozen vectors — SBERT: sentences pass through the model separately, producing “sentence embeddings u and v… compared using cosine similarity” (SBERT cross-encoder docs · dump). A cross-encoder instead reads both texts at once: “we pass both sentences simultaneously to the Transformer network. It produces then an output value between 0 and 1” — and crucially “does not produce a sentence embedding.” Joint attention over the pair lets it judge relevance far more accurately than two independently-compressed vectors ever can.

The catch is cost: with nothing cacheable, every (query, candidate) pair is a fresh model forward pass. SBERT’s scale example: scoring all pairs among 10k sentences ≈ “50 Million sentence combinations… about 65 hours” for a cross-encoder versus ~5 seconds to embed the same set with a bi-encoder (same dump). Hence the universal pattern, verbatim from SBERT: “First, you use an efficient Bi-Encoder to retrieve e.g. the top-100 most similar sentences for a query. Then, you use a Cross-Encoder to re-rank these 100 hits.” Cheap-recall-then-expensive-precision. Your lab stops after stage one — RRF’s output goes straight to the screen. What a reranker would judge that your current scoring can’t (Q17’s seed): BM25 sees token overlap, cosine sees direction between two independently summarized vectors, but only a cross-encoder reads the query against the document’s actual text. Whether that’s worth the latency on your corpus is rung-3’s measured experiment — this rung only needs you to command the concept. (Forward pointers, parked to rung-3: Clavié’s “MVP++” pattern, rerankers library, measured rerank lift.)

Chunking: what “one note = one vector” preserves and destroys

Chunking is deciding what unit of text gets its own vector. Your lab’s answer today: the whole note — title, summary, metadata, and body joined into one string (src/parse.ts:48-50) — with a hard cap: anything past 8,000 characters is silently cut from the embedding (with a warning at index time), because “gemini-embedding-001 takes up to ~2048 tokens” (src/parse.ts:47-58).

Why unit size matters (Pinecone, Chunking Strategies · dump, excerpt+reconstruction): a single vector must represent everything in its text — a long, multi-topic document averages into a vector that matches no specific query sharply (that dilution mechanism is the dump’s labelled teaching gloss, not Pinecone’s own wording) — and over-limit text is simply “truncated, or thrown away.” Small chunks (~128–256 tokens) give sharp, granular matches but lose surrounding context; large chunks (~512–1024) keep context but risk dilution; overlap preserves ideas cut at boundaries at the cost of duplicate tokens. The page’s serviceable heuristic: “if the chunk of text makes sense without the surrounding context to a human, it will make sense to the language model as well,” and its honest bottom line — “there’s no one-size-fits-all solution.”

Two lab-specific consequences you’ll demonstrate in the build:

  1. The truncation asymmetry (app fact, verified in code): FTS5 indexes the full body (upsertNote writes n.body into notes_fts — src/db.ts:110-114), but the embedding sees only the first 8,000 chars of the joined text. Content in a long note’s tail is findable by keyword search and invisible to semantic search. That’s a stage-attribution trap: a “semantic search missed it” symptom whose root cause is corpus processing, not retrieval — exactly the distinction Q10 will formalize.
  2. Your embedText is a proto-enrichment (local observation): prepending title/summary/tags to the body before embedding is a home-grown version of the context-prepending idea that rung-3’s Contextual Retrieval source systematizes. You already do a naive form of it — worth knowing when you read the fancy version.
